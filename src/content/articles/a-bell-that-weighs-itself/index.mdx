Tap a wine glass and then a church bell. Without looking, you know which is which: the small one rings high and the big one rings low. The sound carries information about the thing that made it.

Physicists have used this idea for a long time. The pitch of a vibrating string tells you its tension; the way a building sways after a gust tells an engineer how stiff it is. In 2015 it became possible to do it with a black hole. When two black holes merge, the new one is born lopsided and shakes itself round, sending out gravitational waves that ring like a bell and fade within milliseconds. That ring is called the **ringdown**, and it is enough to weigh the black hole.

This article builds the idea from a mass on a spring up to a black hole, one step at a time.

## Every ring is two numbers

Pull a weight on a spring and let go. It swings back and forth, and friction makes each swing a little smaller than the last. Two numbers describe everything you see: how many swings it makes per second, its <Term id="frequency">frequency</Term> *f*, and how long it takes for the swing to shrink by a factor *e* ≈ 2.72, its <Term id="damping-time">damping time</Term> τ.

Move the sliders. A heavier weight swings more slowly; a stiffer spring swings faster; more friction makes the ring die sooner without changing its pitch much.

The two numbers combine into a third that says how bell-like the ring is: the <Term id="quality-factor">quality factor</Term> *Q* = π *f* τ. It counts, roughly, how many oscillations the ring lasts. A good bell has a *Q* in the thousands. A black hole, as we will see, has a *Q* of about 2 to 4: it rings for only a few cycles before it is silent.

## Why a bigger bell rings lower

Before any equations, there is a way to see why size sets pitch. A black hole in general relativity has no parts, no material, no stiffness: the only things that can set its ring are its mass *M*, the gravitational constant *G* and the speed of light *c*. There is exactly one way to combine them into a time:

<Pull>
  *GM*/*c*³, about <em>{(T_SUN * 1e6).toFixed(2)} microseconds for every solar mass.</em>
</Pull>

So every time in the ring, its period and its damping time, must be a number times *GM*/*c*³, and every frequency a number divided by it. Double the mass and every time doubles: the black hole rings an octave lower and fades half as fast. The numbers in front depend only on how fast the black hole spins, which has no units.

## Only mass and spin

A church bell's ring depends on its shape, its metal, its temperature, a crack you cannot see. A black hole is simpler than any bell. General relativity says that once it has settled down, a black hole is described completely by its mass and its <Term id="spin">spin</Term> χ (it could also carry electric charge, but real ones are neutral). Every other detail of the stars that made it is gone. This is the **no-hair** property, and the solution that describes such a black hole was found by Roy Kerr in 1963.

That makes the ring a fingerprint. Each tone of it, a <Term id="qnm">quasinormal mode</Term>, has a frequency and a damping time fixed entirely by *M* and χ. Measure one tone, two numbers, and you can solve for the two unknowns. Spin changes the tone's *Q*, from about {q0.toFixed(1)} for a black hole that does not spin to {q9.toFixed(1)} for one spinning at χ = 0.95, so *Q* tells you the spin, and then the frequency tells you the mass.

## The bell that is far away

There is one catch, and it matters for everything that follows. The black holes LIGO hears are hundreds of millions of light-years away, and while the waves travelled the universe expanded, stretching them. Every time in the signal arrives longer by a factor 1 + *z*, where *z* is the <Term id="redshift">redshift</Term>.

Stretching every time by 1 + *z* is exactly what a black hole 1 + *z* times heavier would do. So the mass you read from a ring in the detector is the **detector-frame** mass, (1 + *z*) *M*. To know the real, **source-frame** mass, you must know the distance and divide. The spin needs no correction: stretching changes *f* and τ together and leaves *Q* alone. Move the redshift slider above and watch the mass change while the spin stays put.

## The size of the horizon

Mass and spin also fix the size of the black hole's event horizon, the surface from which nothing returns. Its <Term id="horizon-area">area</Term> is *A* = 8π(*GM*/*c*²)²(1 + √(1 − χ²)): it grows with the square of the mass and shrinks a little with spin. The readout above shows it in square kilometres.

In 1971 Stephen Hawking proved that in a merger the total horizon area can only grow: the final black hole's horizon is larger than the two horizons that made it. Measuring the rings of real black holes lets that be checked with data, and in 2025 the loud signal GW250114 did so with high confidence (LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA, 2025). How much such a check really tests is a question the ringdown track takes up.

## What the ring cannot tell you

A ring measures the black hole only if what you fit is really its ring. Right after the merger the signal is not yet a clean set of tones, and the tones that fade fastest, the overtones, overlap with the one you are fitting. Fit too early and the answer drifts; fit too late and the ring is lost in noise. That tension is where real analyses become hard, and where the next articles go.
