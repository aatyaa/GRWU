The Shape of Error

How do you find what you cannot see?

By Attia A. Gadallah·27 September 2026· 25 min read· preview

Every detector ever built records the same thing: the quantity you want, plus everything else. A gravitational-wave detector records a ripple in spacetime plus seismic rumble, laser flicker and the thermal jitter of its mirrors. The ripple is a hundred times smaller than the rest.

So how do you find something you cannot see? The answer starts two centuries earlier, with a planet that went missing.

A planet, lost

On the first night of 1801, Giuseppe Piazzi spotted a faint moving point in the sky over Palermo that belonged to no catalogue. He followed it for 41 nights. Then it drifted too close to the Sun and the glare swallowed it.

Piazzi had seen just nine degrees of its orbit. To find it again, someone had to predict where the entire orbit went from that sliver alone, and every number in the sliver was measured by eye, through a telescope, slightly wrong.

The trouble was not too little data. No orbit passes through all the observations, because each carries its own small error. Pick three observations and you get one orbit; pick three others and you get a different one. The astronomers of 1801 had no principled way to choose, their predictions scattered across the sky, and the planet stayed lost.

Gauss’s rule

A twenty-four-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss stopped arguing about which observations to trust. Instead he defined, once and for all, what makes one curve better than another. Scroll through his rule.

total of the squares 5.59

Here are the observations, laid out in time. Each is a little off, and nobody knows by how much.

Draw any candidate through them. This line is just a guess, and so far there is nothing to say it is a bad one.

Now measure how far the candidate misses each observation: the dashed lines.

Square each miss and add the squares up. That total is the candidate’s score. A few large misses cost more than many small ones, so no observation can be ignored.

Gauss’s rule: the best candidate is the one with the smallest total. Every observation contributes, none is thrown away, and the answer no longer depends on who is choosing. This is least squares.

In December 1801 the astronomer von Zach pointed his telescope where Gauss’s orbit said the planet would be, and Ceres was there. Now it is your turn: move the line until the shaded area is as small as you can make it, then let Gauss check.

The math: least squares is a right angle
The claim.

The best parameters are the ones that leave the miss perpendicular to everything the model could have produced, and that condition has a closed-form solution.

    Set the derivative to zero. Geometrically: the leftover is orthogonal to every column of A.

  3. θ^=(A⊤A)−1A⊤y\hat\theta = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top} yθ^=(A⊤A)−1A⊤y

    Solved in one line: no search. With a single column of ones, this is the mean.

What it means.

The fit is the foot of the perpendicular from the data to everything the model can reach.

Why squares?

Nothing forces you to square the misses. You could add up the plain distances, or raise them to the fourth power. Each rule is defensible, and each picks a different answer out of the same data.

Here is how different they are. Eight measurements of one quantity, seven that agree and one that does not. Minimising the squares gives the mean, minimising the plain distances gives the median, and minimising the worst miss gives the midrange. Drag the outlier and watch who follows it.

To justify squares over the others, Gauss had to answer a harder question first: what does a measurement error look like?

The shape of error

Before any mathematics, three things are true about mistakes. Small misses are far more common than large ones. Missing high and missing low are equally likely. Enormous misses essentially never happen. Collect enough errors and they trace a shape that everyone who has measured anything has seen.

Gauss ran the argument backwards. Astronomers already trusted the average of repeated measurements, so he asked: which error curve makes the average the most probable answer? Exactly one does, the bell curve, and it has carried his name ever since.

From the bell to least squares

Follow the bell curve through and least squares stops being a choice. The chance of one measurement missing by r falls off as the bell. Independent measurements multiply, and multiplying exponentials adds their exponents, so the probability of all of them together depends on one number: the sum of the squared misses.

Least squares was never a convention. It falls out of the shape of error.

    Largest probability is smallest χ², and a good fit has χ² close to the number of points minus the number of parameters.

What it means.

This holds because the noise is Gaussian. Change the noise and the right rule changes with it, which is why so much of gravitational-wave analysis is about whether detector noise really is Gaussian.

Look at what is left

A fit always returns an answer. It never refuses, so the fit alone cannot tell you whether the model was any good. The check is to subtract the fitted curve from the data and look at the leftovers, the residuals. If the model captured everything real, they are just noise, with no shape you can point at.

If you can still see a pattern in what is left over, it belongs to something your model does not contain.

That sentence is the whole of the next story. When the signal is a hundred times fainter than the noise and the model is a template of two black holes merging, a missing piece does not show itself so politely.
