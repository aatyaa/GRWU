Almost None of It Is About Gravitational Waves

What do you actually have to know to do this work?

By Attia A. Gadallah·27 September 2026· 25 min read· preview

People assume the barrier to working on gravitational waves is the physics. It is not. A working week here is spent writing Python, reading somebody else’s code, waiting for jobs on a cluster, and arguing about whether a number is real.

This article is the map of what the work is actually made of. Of its fourteen leaves, only two are specific to this field, and they are the fastest part to learn. Both get a close look below, because they are also the parts that are rarely explained: the waveform models and the field’s own libraries.

The map

Everything else transfers. Fourier analysis, probability, linear algebra, Python, the habit of distrusting your own best fit: they are the same in climate science, medical imaging and machine learning. That is good news twice over. The field is more open to newcomers than its reputation, and nothing you learn here is wasted if you leave.

Three pieces of advice follow from the map.

Do not skip Fourier

The noise spectrum, whitening and the matched filter look like three techniques. They are one Fourier argument in three different costumes. Each starts by writing the data as a sum of frequencies. Each then treats every frequency on its own, which is allowed only because stationary noise makes different frequencies independent. Two of them finish by transforming back.

Once that is yours, Hidden in the Noise reads as one idea instead of three.

Learn statistics properly, once

Not recipes. The distinction that stays with you for a career is between a likelihood and a posterior. The likelihood says how probable your data would be for each value of a parameter. It is a function of the parameter, but it is not a probability distribution over it. The posterior is: the likelihood multiplied by what you believed beforehand, the prior.

With a flat prior, the posterior is the likelihood, renormalised. A hunch pulls the answer toward the hunch, and more data pulls it back. A physical bound cuts the posterior off, and that is legitimate: a black hole cannot outweigh what formed it. But a fixed value is an infinitely strong prior. The data can say anything and the answer does not move. That is why setting a black hole’s spin to zero in an analysis is not “ignoring spin”: it is asserting, with certainty, that the spin is zero.

The first gold leaf: waveform models

A waveform model is a function. Give it the parameters of a source and it returns the signal the detectors should record. For two black holes that means fifteen numbers: two masses, three spin components for each object, the distance, two angles for the orientation of the orbit, two for the position on the sky, the arrival time and the phase.

The search slides a bank of these templates along the data. Parameter estimation evaluates the model millions of times, once for every point the sampler visits. So a model has to be right and it has to be fast, and those two pull against each other.

Three regimes, three theories

No single calculation covers a whole merger. While the black holes are far apart and slow, the inspiral can be expanded in powers of their speed over the speed of light: post-Newtonian theory. In the last few orbits nothing is small any more, and only numerical relativity, solving Einstein’s equations on a supercomputer, can follow the merger. After it, the single remnant settles like a struck bell, and black-hole perturbation theory predicts the tones of its ringdown from its mass and spin alone.

And general relativity has a remarkable property: in empty space, the only scale is the mass. One waveform serves every binary with the same mass ratio and spins. Double the total mass and the whole signal plays twice as slowly, an octave lower. Choose the mass and watch what the detector actually hears.

A light binary spends seconds and hundreds of cycles of inspiral inside the detector’s band, where post-Newtonian theory is at home. For a heavy one, the inspiral ends almost as soon as it becomes audible. What the detector hears is mostly merger and ringdown: the part only numerical relativity can compute. Which regime a model must get right depends on the source.

What it means.

One simulation at one mass is every mass. This is why numerical-relativity catalogues are labelled by mass ratio and spins, and why a template bank’s density depends so strongly on the total mass.

Five families

Every waveform model in use is a compromise between the three regimes, and the families differ in where they put the compromise.

Post-Newtonian models, such as TaylorF2, are closed-form expansions. They take microseconds, are accurate while the objects are far apart, and say nothing about the merger. They matter most for neutron-star binaries, which spend a minute or more in the band.

Numerical relativity solves the full equations. One simulation takes weeks on a supercomputer and covers only the last tens of orbits of one particular binary; catalogues such as SXS hold a few thousand of them. It is the ground truth everything else is checked against, and far too slow to use directly.

Effective-one-body models, the SEOBNR family, map the two-body problem onto one body moving in a deformed black-hole spacetime, gather in the post-Newtonian information, and calibrate the pieces nobody can calculate against numerical relativity.

Phenomenological models, the IMRPhenom family, fit smooth formulas across inspiral, merger and ringdown, calibrated to effective-one-body and numerical-relativity waveforms. They are the fastest complete models, which is why so many catalogue analyses use them.

Surrogates, such as NRSur7dq4, interpolate directly between numerical-relativity simulations. Within the range the simulations cover, they are the most faithful fast models. Outside it, they do not apply.

In code you choose one by name, the approximant: IMRPhenomT, SEOBNRv4_opt, IMRPhenomXP. Names with a P include spin precession; PHM adds higher harmonics.

Where the models are still wrong

The frontier is where the physics gets harder: spins tilted so that the orbit precesses, unequal masses that make the higher harmonics loud, orbits that are still eccentric, and neutron stars whose matter deforms under tides. Each is included in some models and approximated in others. Two good models can disagree about the same signal, and when they do, the difference moves the answer. That is the almost-right template of What the Wrong Model Knows, and it is why careful analyses repeat a measurement with more than one model and report the spread.

The approximant is not a detail. It is the strongest assumption in the analysis.

The second gold leaf: gwpy · PyCBC · Bilby

Learn the packages last. A library is a weekend; the thinking underneath it is years, and it transfers to any field that fits models to noisy data. But the weekend does have to happen, so here it is.

Everything sits on numerical Python. LALSuite, written in C by the collaborations, holds the waveform models and the response of each detector. Above it, gwosc asks the public archive what exists. gwpy fetches strain, plots it and computes its spectra. PyCBC builds templates and filters data; it grew out of the search pipelines themselves. Bilby turns a waveform and some data into a likelihood and hands it to a sampler, usually dynesty. You will use gwpy and PyCBC side by side: they do overlapping jobs with different objects, and converting between them is routine.

Read the traps again. Almost none of them is about a library. Crop what your filter corrupted; estimate the noise robustly; do not mistake a picture for a detection; know what your prior asserts. The library only decides where you type it.

The warning everybody hits first

Importing pycbc or lalsuite prints a wall of Wswiglal-redir-stdio messages. They are harmless, and the official notebooks silence them on their first line.

From here to real work

Nobody hands you a problem. You take one, and the route there has four stops.

Reproduce something already known. The strain data for every published detection is public. Download an event, whiten it, filter it against a template, and watch a signal come out of noise you cannot see through. You are not discovering anything: you are proving to yourself that the machinery is real.

Then break it on purpose. Build a signal whose answer you chose, and recover it. Now make the template slightly wrong, drop a component or shift a frequency, and measure how far the answer moves. This is where you stop following tutorials.

Contribute to the tools. The whole analysis stack is open source and imperfect. Fix a bug, add the test that should have existed, improve the docstring that misled you. It is small and real, and it puts you inside the community instead of reading about it.

Ask something nobody has checked. Not a grand question: a small one with a definite answer. Almost every real paper is one honest small question, answered carefully.

Two honest notes

The last stop is far easier inside a research group, and that is what a master’s or a PhD is really for: supervision, a community, and permission to spend years on one question. Summer research programmes and the collaborations’ open data workshops are the usual door in. And if you leave for industry, none of it is wasted. Inference from noisy data under a model you are not sure of is the whole of applied machine learning, described in different words.
