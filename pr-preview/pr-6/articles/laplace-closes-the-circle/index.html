Laplace Closes the Circle

Why does the bell curve appear at all, and what does the data really tell you?

By Attia A. Gadallah·28 September 2026· 25 min read· preview

The Shape of Error ended with Gauss’s rule justified by the bell curve: if errors follow the bell, the most probable answer is the one with the smallest sum of squares. Two loose ends were left hanging. Gauss had assumed the average was the right answer in order to show that the bell was the right curve, an argument that runs in a circle. And the probability he maximised answers a question nobody actually asks.

This article ties both ends. It is the rest of the story: why only the bell will do, why least squares survives even without it, how Laplace explained where the bell comes from, and how he turned Gauss’s question the right way round.

Only the bell will do

Astronomers trusted one thing without proof: when you measure the same quantity several times, you take the average. Gauss took that as given and ran the argument backwards. What would the error curve have to be for the average to be the most probable answer, for every sample anyone could ever take?

The answer turns on the error curve’s score: the slope of its logarithm, which says how fast the probability of a miss falls as the miss grows. Gauss’s demand forces the score to scale in step with the miss. Double the miss, double the score. Only a straight line through the origin does that, and only one error law has a straight score.

Integrate a straight score and you get the bell. It is not an assumption you are asked to swallow: it is the unique answer to a question everyone had already answered by instinct. But notice what was assumed on the way. Gauss used the average to justify the bell. Someone still had to justify one of them without the other.

The math: the bell is the only law whose score is a straight line
The claim.

Demand that the arithmetic mean be the most probable value for every sample you could ever take. That demand alone forces the error distribution to be Gaussian. Not merely allows it: forces it.

Assumean error density g, smooth and the same for every measurement, and Gauss’s axiom that the mean is the most probable value.
  1. ψ(u)=dduln⁡g(u)\psi(u) = \frac{d}{du} \ln g(u)ψ(u)=dud​lng(u)

    Name the log-derivative of the density: the score.

  2. ∑iψ(xi−xˉ)=0\sum_i \psi(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0i∑​ψ(xi​−xˉ)=0

    The most probable value makes this sum vanish, and Gauss demands that value be the mean, for every sample.

  3. ψ(m u)=m ψ(u)\psi(m\,u) = m\,\psi(u)ψ(mu)=mψ(u)

    Feed it a sample of equal readings with one odd one out, and the condition collapses to this, for every whole number m and every u.

  4. ψ(u)=c u\psi(u) = c\,uψ(u)=cu

    A continuous function that stretches exactly in step with its input is a straight line through the origin. There is no other.

  5. ln⁡g(u)=c2u2+const  ⇒  g(u)∝e−u2/2σ2\ln g(u) = \tfrac{c}{2}u^2 + \text{const} \;\Rightarrow\; g(u) \propto e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}lng(u)=2c​u2+const⇒g(u)∝e−u2/2σ2

    Integrate. For the density to be normalisable c must be negative: write it as −1/σ².

What it means.

The characterisation is Gauss’s own (Theoria Motus, 1809, §177). In modern terms: the mean is the maximum-likelihood estimate for every sample size if and only if the errors are Gaussian.

Least squares without the bell

There is a second road to least squares that never mentions the bell at all. Gauss found it himself in 1821, and Andrey Markov restated it eighty years later; it now carries both their names. Assume only that the errors average to zero, have the same size, and do not correlate with one another. Their shape can be anything. Then among all the estimates that are linear in the data and right on average, least squares has the smallest spread.

See it happen. Below, a straight line is measured at ten points with noise, four thousand times over, and three honest estimators of its slope go to work on every dataset. Change the shape of the noise.

Least squares, the difference between the two halves of the data, the first and last points alone: all three centre on the truth for every noise shape, and least squares is always the narrowest. What the bell buys on top of this is stronger: with Gaussian noise, least squares is the best of all estimators, not only the linear ones.

The math: least squares wins even when the errors are not Gaussian
The claim.

Among all estimators that are linear in the data and unbiased, least squares has the smallest spread, with no assumption about the shape of the errors.

Assumeerrors with mean zero, equal variance, and no correlation between them. Nothing about their shape.
  1. ⟨n⟩=0,Cov(n)=σ2I\langle n \rangle = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(n) = \sigma^2 I⟨n⟩=0,Cov(n)=σ2I

    The whole assumption. What is absent matters: no density, no bell, not even independence.

  2. θ~=C y,unbiased for every θ\tilde\theta = C\,y, \quad \text{unbiased for every } \thetaθ~=Cy,unbiased for every θ

    Any rival estimator that is linear in the data and right on average. Least squares is one member of this wide class.

  3. Cov(θ~)−Cov(θ^)⪰0\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde\theta) - \mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta) \succeq 0Cov(θ~)−Cov(θ^)⪰0

    For every such rival, the difference in covariance is positive semi-definite: none spreads less than least squares.

  4. θ^ is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator\hat\theta \text{ is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator}θ^ is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator

    This is the Gauss–Markov theorem.

What it means.

Least squares is not only a consequence of Gaussian noise; it is the best linear unbiased choice under far weaker conditions. Aitken later extended the theorem to errors of different sizes that correlate, and that extension is whitening: see Hidden in the Noise.

Laplace: why the curve is there at all

Gauss showed what the error curve must be if the average is the best answer. Pierre-Simon Laplace showed why the curve appears at all, assuming nothing about the average.

His observation: a measurement error is never one thing. It is hundreds of tiny disturbances piled on top of each other: a draught across the lens, a fraction of a degree of warmth, the tremble in a hand, the grain of the eye, a floorboard settling. And it does not matter what shape each disturbance has. Add enough of them together and the total becomes a bell. Try it.

A die, a lopsided spread, a coin that lands on one of two values: by a handful of pieces the totals already trace the bell, and nobody put it there. This is the central limit theorem, and it is why the same curve turns up in human heights, in exam marks and in the noise of a gravitational-wave detector.

When this argument fails, and it does

The theorem needs the disturbances to be many, small and independent, with a finite spread. Choose the heavy-tailed pieces: now and then one of them is enormous on its own, the totals never settle into a bell, and about a tenth of them land beyond four spreads, where a bell puts almost none. Real detectors suffer exactly this, often enough to have a name for it: a glitch. None of Gauss’s mathematics applies to it.

The math: why sums forget the shape they were made of
The claim.

Add up many small independent contributions and the total tends to a bell, whatever the pieces looked like. The reason is visible in one line of algebra, if you look at the right function.

Assumecontributions that are independent and identically distributed, with mean zero and a finite variance σ². Finiteness is the condition that matters.
  1. φ(t)=⟨eitX⟩\varphi(t) = \langle e^{itX} \rangleφ(t)=⟨eitX⟩

    The characteristic function: the average of a rotating phase. It carries exactly the same information as the distribution.

  2. φX1+X2(t)=φX1(t) φX2(t)\varphi_{X_1 + X_2}(t) = \varphi_{X_1}(t)\,\varphi_{X_2}(t)φX1​+X2​​(t)=φX1​​(t)φX2​​(t)

    Adding independent variables multiplies their characteristic functions. A hard operation becomes an easy one.

  3. φ(u)=1−σ2u22+o(u2)\varphi(u) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2 u^2}{2} + o(u^2)φ(u)=1−2σ2u2​+o(u2)

    Expand for small u. The mean is zero, so the variance is the first thing that survives: the only feature of the shape that matters.

  4. φZn(t)=(1−t22n+o ⁣(1n)) ⁣n→e−t2/2\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \left(1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right)^{\!n} \to e^{-t^2/2}φZn​​(t)=(1−2nt2​+o(n1​))n→e−t2/2

    Scale the sum by 1/(σ√n) and let n grow: the ordinary limit that defines e.

  5. e−t2/2 is the characteristic function of the belle^{-t^2/2} \text{ is the characteristic function of the bell}e−t2/2 is the characteristic function of the bell

    And a characteristic function determines its distribution. (The convergence step is Lévy’s continuity theorem.)

What it means.

The proof fails exactly where the variance is infinite. Distributions with tails that heavy do not converge to a bell, and their sums keep producing outliers however many terms you add. That is not a curiosity: it is what a detector glitch is.

Gauss answered the wrong question

The likelihood tells you: given this model, how probable was the data I got? Read that again. It treats the model as settled and the data as uncertain, which is exactly backwards. You already have the data. It is sitting on your disk; it is not uncertain at all. What you do not know is the model. The question you actually want answered is the other one: given the data I got, how probable is this model?

Laplace was the first to take that flip seriously, and to give a rule for performing it.

p(model∣data)  ∝  p(data∣model)×p(model)\htmlData{term=posterior}{p(\text{model} \mid \text{data})} \;\propto\; \htmlData{term=likelihood}{p(\text{data} \mid \text{model})} \times \htmlData{term=prior}{p(\text{model})}p(model∣data)∝p(data∣model)×p(model)
The math: Bayes' rule is one sentence about a rectangle
The claim.

Turning the probability of the data into the probability of the model is not deep. A joint probability can be cut two ways, and the two cuts must agree.

Assumetwo events, and the definition of conditional probability. That is all.
  1. p(A,B)=p(A∣B) p(B)p(A, B) = p(A \mid B)\,p(B)p(A,B)=p(A∣B)p(B)

    Cut it one way: how often B happens, times how often A happens when it does.

  2. p(A,B)=p(B∣A) p(A)p(A, B) = p(B \mid A)\,p(A)p(A,B)=p(B∣A)p(A)

    Cut it the other way. Same square, same corner, same area.

  3. p(A∣B) p(B)=p(B∣A) p(A)p(A \mid B)\,p(B) = p(B \mid A)\,p(A)p(A∣B)p(B)=p(B∣A)p(A)

    Both equal the same thing, so they equal each other. This line is the whole theorem.

  4. p(h∣d)=p(d∣h) p(h)p(d)p(h \mid d) = \frac{p(d \mid h)\,p(h)}{p(d)}p(h∣d)=p(d)p(d∣h)p(h)​

    Divide, and rename: h for the model, d for the data you already hold.

What it means.

Nothing controversial has happened. What is controversial is the use: to get p(h | d) you must supply p(h), a probability for the model before any data existed. The mathematics is a triviality; the prior is the commitment.

When the prior saves you

Suppose the data are poor, and the fit, left to itself, returns its best answer: the object has a negative mass. The likelihood has no objection. It only knows how to compare curves to points, and that curve fits the points best; physics is not among its concerns.

The prior does object. Mass cannot be negative, so the prior is zero everywhere below zero, and multiplying by zero ends the argument.

The prior does not adjust the fit. It deletes the region the fit was never entitled to visit.

A prior is never free

Deleting a negative mass feels like common sense. But nothing in the mathematics tells common sense apart from what you were hoping to find. The prior is whatever you assert before looking, and the machinery obeys it without comment. Assert little and the data decides. Assert a lot and the data is merely nudged. Assert enough and the data cannot reach the answer at all: you get back what you put in, dressed as a measurement, with error bars.

The strongest prior in the room is the one nobody counts as a prior: the shape of the model itself.

Choosing a template, a straight line, a circle, a single ringing tone, declares that the signal looks like that and like nothing else. It is the fixed prior above, and it is where What the Wrong Model Knows happens.

One more thing Gauss invented

Ceres’s orbit is not a straight line in its parameters. It needs six orbital elements, and its position in the sky depends on them in a way no straight line can capture. Gauss’s answer was to pretend the model is straight for one small step, solve least squares for that step, and repeat. It is called Gauss–Newton, and it still fits waveforms to data today.

Here it fits the frequency of a wave to sixty noisy points. The solid curve is how badly each frequency fits; the dashed curve is what the straight-line pretence sees from where you stand.

Start near 3 Hz and the steps walk into the deepest valley. Start near 5 and they settle happily in a shallow one, reporting a tidy answer all the way down. The method finds a valley floor, not necessarily the deepest, which is why real fits start from many places. And it converges just as happily on a wrong model.

The math: a hard problem, solved by doing an easy one repeatedly
Assumea model h(θ) that is smooth but not linear, and a starting guess in the right valley.
  1. h(θ+δθ)≈h(θ)+J δθh(\theta + \delta\theta) \approx h(\theta) + J\,\delta\thetah(θ+δθ)≈h(θ)+Jδθ

    Expand the model about the current guess. To first order any smooth function is a straight one.

  2. J=∂h/∂θJ = \partial h / \partial \thetaJ=∂h/∂θ

    The matrix of first derivatives plays the part the columns of the model played in least squares.

  3. δθ^=(J⊤J)−1J⊤ (y−h(θ))\hat{\delta\theta} = (J^{\top} J)^{-1} J^{\top}\,(y - h(\theta))δθ^=(J⊤J)−1J⊤(y−h(θ))

    So the step is exactly the least-squares solution, applied to what is left over. Take it, and linearise again about the new point.

  4. repeat until δθ^ stops changing\text{repeat until } \hat{\delta\theta} \text{ stops changing}repeat until δθ^ stops changing

    Gauss–Newton.

What it means.

Two warnings that matter later. It finds a valley floor, not necessarily the deepest, which is why a fit with two tones needs several starting points. And it converges happily on a wrong model, reporting a tidy answer the whole way down.

Where the story goes next

Everything so far has one structure. Whatever you record is a sum: the thing you want, plus everything else.

d=h+n\htmlData{term=d}{d} = \htmlData{term=h}{h} + \htmlData{term=n}{n}d=h+n

For Piazzi it was a star’s position plus the tremble of the atmosphere and the limits of his eye. For a gravitational-wave detector it is a ripple in spacetime plus seismic rumble, laser flicker and the thermal jitter of the mirrors. The rest of the story is about the two letters on the right: how to describe n, and what happens when you assume the wrong h.
