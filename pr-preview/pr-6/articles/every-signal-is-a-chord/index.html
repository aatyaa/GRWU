Every Signal Is a Chord

How do you take a signal apart into its notes?

By Attia A. Gadallah·5 October 2026· 20 min read· preview

Play a chord on a piano and your ear does something remarkable: it hears three notes, not one tangled sound. The air carries a single wobble, the sum of the three, and your inner ear takes it apart again by pitch.

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that this is not a trick of hearing but a fact about signals. Any reasonable wiggle, the vibration of a string, the temperature around a ring of iron, the stretch of space as a gravitational wave passes, can be written as a sum of steady sine waves, each with its own frequency, size and starting point. Mathematicians argued about it for decades. Today it is the first tool anyone uses to look at data.

A signal is a sum of tones

Below are three tones and their sum. The top panel is what a detector records: one line, wiggling in time. The bottom panel is its spectrum, which shows how much of each frequency the line contains. Move the sliders and watch the two views change together.

Turn a tone off and its bar disappears; make it quieter and its bar shrinks; slide it and the bar slides. In the time view the tones are all mixed up. In the spectrum they are separate again. That is the whole point of the spectrum: it turns a tangle into a list.

Recording means sampling

A detector does not record a smooth curve. It writes down a number many times a second; at LIGO, 4096 times a second. How often you measure, the sampling rate, sets a limit you cannot escape. In 1928 Harry Nyquist showed, and in 1949 Claude Shannon proved in general, that samples taken fₛ times a second can only describe tones slower than fₛ/2. Anything faster is not lost quietly. It is recorded as a slower tone that was never there.

Before you read on, make a guess.

You record a 45 Hz tone, sampling 64 times a second. What frequency do your samples show?

The red curve is not an approximation of the truth; it fits the samples exactly, and so does the real tone. From the samples alone you cannot tell them apart. This is why detectors filter out frequencies above fₛ/2 before they sample. At 4096 samples a second LIGO can safely record tones up to 2048 Hz, well above the few hundred hertz at which black holes merge and ring.

How long you listen decides what you can hear

The spectrum of a recording is computed with the fast Fourier transform, published by James Cooley and John Tukey in 1965, which made spectra cheap enough to compute for every recording. It turns N samples into the strength of N/2 + 1 evenly spaced frequencies. The spacing between them is fixed by how long the recording lasts: a stretch of T seconds can only resolve frequencies 1/T apart. Two tones closer than that merge into one bump.

This has a sharp consequence for black holes. The ring of a 60 solar-mass black hole lasts only about 11 milliseconds before it fades into the noise, so its spectrum can resolve nothing finer than about 92 Hz. That is one reason ringdowns are usually analysed directly in time rather than through their spectrum.

Cutting a stretch out of a signal

There is a catch in taking a spectrum of a stretch of data. The transform assumes the stretch repeats forever, end joined to start. If a tone does not fit a whole number of times into the stretch, that join is a jump, and a jump contains every frequency at once. The tone’s energy leaks into frequencies far from its own, and that smear can hide a quiet signal next to a loud one.

The cure, studied in detail by Fredric Harris in 1978, is to fade the data in and out before taking the spectrum: multiply it by a window that is small at the ends. Compare the windows below.

The sound of the detector

Now point all of this at a real instrument. The noise of a detector is not one tone but a hiss of every frequency at once, and what matters is how loud the hiss is at each frequency: its power spectral density, usually drawn as its square root, the amplitude spectral density, in units of strain per √Hz.

A single spectrum of noise is itself noisy: each frequency’s value jumps around by as much as its size, however long the recording. In 1967 Peter Welch suggested cutting the data into many overlapping segments, windowing each, and averaging their spectra. Longer segments give finer frequency resolution; more segments give a smoother average. Below is Welch’s method applied to 32 seconds of real LIGO data recorded around the first detection, GW150914.

Three things stand out. The noise is enormous at low frequency, where the ground shakes and the mirrors’ suspensions move. It rises again at high frequency, where light itself is grainy (shot noise). And between them is a valley, a few hundred hertz wide, where the detector listens best: exactly where merging black holes of tens of solar masses sing. The narrow spikes are lines: the mains electricity and its harmonics, and the violin-like vibration of the wires that hold the mirrors (LIGO and Virgo, 2020).

A common mistake

“With a long enough recording, the spectrum of noise becomes exact.”

A longer stretch makes the frequency spacing finer, but each value stays just as uncertain. Only averaging many spectra, as Welch’s method does, makes the estimate steadier. That is why analyses trade resolution for steadiness by choosing the segment length.

Why the spectrum of the noise matters

A signal at a frequency where the noise is loud is hard to see; the same signal in the quiet valley stands out. So the first thing every search does is divide the data by the noise at each frequency, putting every frequency on an equal footing. That step is called whitening, and it is where the next article, on finding a signal hidden in the noise, begins.
