Only Ever Forwards

Why can a black hole's horizon only grow, and what does it take to hear two of them become one?

By Attia A. Gadallah·22 October 2026· 34 min read· preview

Mass, Spin and Nothing Else ended on two numbers. However complicated the star that collapsed, the hole it leaves is fixed by its mass and its spin, and nothing else about it can be measured from outside. This article is about the two places where that simplicity runs into something deeper. The first is a single surprising law: a horizon’s area can only grow, and it behaves exactly like entropy. The second is what happens when two of these simple objects collide, and what it took to work out the sound they make.

The area can only grow

In 1971 Stephen Hawking proved a theorem about the horizon itself. Whatever happens to a black hole, as long as the matter around it has positive energy, the total area of its horizon never decreases. Drop matter in and the area rises. Take energy out of a spinning hole by the Penrose process, and its mass and spin both fall, but the area still rises. Merge two holes, and the new horizon is larger than the two old ones together.

The area of a horizon grows as the square of the mass, so this is a strong statement about mergers. Two holes of 10 solar masses cannot become one of 20 that radiates nothing and then shrinks back: that one would have four times the area of either. More usefully, they cannot radiate everything either. The final area must be at least the two first areas together, and that sets a floor on the final mass.

Before you read on, make a guess.

Two black holes of 10 solar masses, neither spinning, merge. What is the most of their 20 solar masses the merger could turn into gravitational waves without breaking the area theorem?

Spin makes the floor higher. A spinning hole has a smaller horizon than a still one of the same mass, so to keep the area, a spinning remnant must keep more of the mass. Every merger in the universe, from the lightest to the heaviest, is bracketed this way by geometry alone.

What it means.

The ceiling on any merger’s output comes from geometry alone, before any detail of the collision is known. A spinning remnant has a smaller horizon for its mass, so it must keep more of the mass, and the ceiling drops.

Four laws in the same order

By 1973 James Bardeen, Brandon Carter and Hawking had found that black holes obey four laws, and that the four laws had a familiar look. Surface gravity, the pull felt at the horizon by an observer far away holding a string, is the same all over a settled horizon. A change of mass splits into a part proportional to the change of area and a part that goes into spin. The area never decreases. And no finite process can bring the surface gravity to zero.

The three authors were careful to call this an analogy and nothing more. A black hole was, by definition, something that absorbs everything and emits nothing. Its temperature must therefore be exactly zero, and a body at zero temperature cannot take part in thermodynamics in the usual way. The match had to be formal. Their paper says so.

A cup of tea and a graduate student

At Princeton, John Wheeler put a puzzle to his graduate student Jacob Bekenstein. Pour a cup of hot tea into a black hole. The tea had entropy, and now it is gone from the universe outside. The second law of thermodynamics, perhaps the most secure law in physics, says the total entropy of the universe never falls. Has it just been broken?

Bekenstein took the analogy literally. In 1972 and 1973 he proposed that a horizon’s area is its entropy, up to a constant, and that a generalised second law holds: the entropy outside, plus the entropy of the horizons, never decreases. His argument was about information. Lower one bit into a hole as gently as possible and the horizon still grows by a fixed minimum, of the order of the Planck area, about 10⁻⁷⁰ m². So the entropy counts the area in those units, not the volume inside, which is not even well defined.

Most people thought he was wrong, and Hawking was among them. A body with entropy has a temperature, a body with a temperature radiates, and black holes do not radiate. Hawking set out to show this properly. What he found instead, in 1974, was that they do.

He set out to refute the idea and ended up proving it.

Black holes are not black

Hawking applied quantum field theory to the curved spacetime outside a horizon. In quantum theory the vacuum is not empty; it is full of fluctuations, and what counts as a particle depends on who is counting. An observer falling freely through the horizon sees nothing special there. An observer holding still far outside sees a steady glow, with the spectrum of a hot body at a definite temperature:

TH=ℏc38πGMkBT_\mathrm{H} = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8 \pi G M k_\mathrm{B}}TH​=8πGMkB​ℏc3​

Every constant of nature that matters here is in it. The ħ says it is a purely quantum effect: it vanishes if quantum mechanics is switched off. The M in the denominator says something stranger. Heavier holes are colder.

The numbers are tiny. A hole of one solar mass is at about 62 billionths of a kelvin; the hole GW250114 left, at 0.8 billionths, is colder still. The sky they sit in is filled with the light left over from the Big Bang, at 2.7 K. So every black hole yet found absorbs far more of that light than it emits, and is growing, not shrinking. Only a hole lighter than about 4.5 × 10²² kg, roughly the mass of the Moon, is hotter than the sky today.

Left alone in an empty universe, a hole would evaporate. Counting only the light it gives off, a solar-mass hole lasts about 2 × 10⁶⁷ years; the other massless particles shorten that by a factor of a few. A hole of about 2 × 10¹¹ kg would be finishing now, after the age of the universe. Such small holes could only have been made in the very early universe, and none has been seen.

A common mistake

“Hawking radiation is pairs of particles appearing at the horizon, one falling in and the other escaping.”

That picture is Hawking’s own, from a popular account, and he later said it was only a heuristic. The calculation is about how the same quantum field is split into particles by two different observers. What one calls empty, the other sees as a thermal glow. Nothing in the real calculation happens at a point on the horizon.

What it means.

No particle and no pair appears in the argument. The temperature comes from asking the geometry to be smooth, and the factor of one quarter is not fitted to anything.

Entropy is a count

Ludwig Boltzmann’s formula, carved on his grave in Vienna, says what entropy is:

S=kBln⁡ΩS = k_\mathrm{B} \ln \OmegaS=kB​lnΩ

Here Ω is the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. A gas in a box has an enormous Ω because nobody can see the molecules. The second law is then less a law of nature than a statement about counting: things drift towards the descriptions that more arrangements share, because there are more of them.

Bekenstein and Hawking’s result is

S=kBA4ℓP2,ℓP=ℏGc3S = \frac{k_\mathrm{B} A}{4 \ell_\mathrm{P}^2}, \qquad \ell_\mathrm{P} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^3}}S=4ℓP2​kB​A​,ℓP​=c3ℏG​​

with ℓP, the Planck length, about 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m. Four constants meet in it: kB from heat, c from relativity, G from gravity and ħ from quantum mechanics. No other result we are confident of contains all four. And it says something nobody expected. A hole of one solar mass has an entropy of about 1 × 10⁷⁷ kB, so about e to the power 10⁷⁷ arrangements. General relativity says it has exactly two properties. One of those two sentences is incomplete, and finding out which is most of what a quantum theory of gravity has to do.

Any candidate for that theory is therefore set the same exam: reproduce A/4 by counting something. In 1996 Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa did it in string theory for a special kind of hole, extremal and supersymmetric, by counting configurations of branes, and got A/4 including the quarter. It remains the theory’s most convincing result, for holes quite unlike those in the sky. Loop quantum gravity counts the ways a horizon can be punctured by its quantum geometry and gets A/4 once one free parameter is fixed, which some regard as fitting rather than deriving. And the holographic principle of Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind turns the result around: if the most entropy a region can hold is set by its boundary’s area, then a volume of space might be fully described by what lives on its surface. Juan Maldacena found an exact example of such a duality in 1997, in a kind of spacetime ours is not.

Entropy does not add up

For a hole without spin the entropy grows as the square of the mass. That breaks a rule ordinary matter always follows. Put two identical boxes of gas side by side and the entropy doubles. Merge two identical black holes and, before anything is radiated, the entropy quadruples.

So for a merger, the area theorem is less a deep fact about dynamics than a consequence of this curve bending upwards. It is the same geometry seen a second time.

GW250114, in entropy

On 14 January 2025 both LIGO detectors recorded the loudest merger yet heard. Two black holes of 33.6 and 32.2 solar masses, with small spins, became one of 62.7 solar masses spinning at 0.68 (LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA, 2025). The collaboration used it to test the area theorem directly, measuring the areas before the merger from the inspiral and after it from the ringing, and the area grew, with high confidence.

From those numbers the entropy follows. Taking the first two holes as not spinning, which their small spins allow, the horizons covered about 238,000 km² before the merger and about 373,000 km² after. In entropy, that is 2.27 × 10⁸⁰ kB before and 3.57 × 10⁸⁰ after.

About 3.1 solar masses of energy left as gravitational waves. It is natural to think the entropy went with them. It did not. Most of that energy left in the last few cycles, at a couple of hundred hertz. At 200 Hz it is about 4 × 10⁷⁸ quanta of the gravitational field. Radiation is most disordered when it is thermal, and thermal radiation carries about 3.6 kB per quantum, so even at its most disordered the radiation could hold only about 2 × 10⁷⁹ kB, around a tenth of what the horizons gained.

And the waves were nothing like thermal. A chirp is coherent: one phase and one frequency at each instant, predictable to a fraction of a cycle over many cycles. That is why matched filtering can find it at all. A coherent wave has almost no entropy. The merger sent its energy out in the most orderly form available and kept the entropy behind the horizon.

Energy left in the waves. The entropy stayed behind the horizon.

Across the universe the same thing holds on a vaster scale. Black holes hold almost all of the entropy in the observable universe: about 10¹⁰⁴ kB in the supermassive holes at the centres of galaxies, against about 10⁸⁹ in the light left over from the Big Bang (Egan and Lineweaver, 2010). Sagittarius A* alone, at 4.3 million solar masses, holds about 2 × 10⁹⁰. Roger Penrose has drawn the conclusion: if gravity’s entropy is so large now, the smooth, early universe must have started in a state of extraordinarily low entropy. That low beginning is where the arrow of time comes from, and every merger spends a little more of it, for good.

A strange kind of thermodynamics

A black hole breaks most of the assumptions thermodynamics is built on. Its entropy grows with area, not volume, so it is not proportional to the amount of stuff. Joining two of them gives more than the sum. And because its temperature falls as its mass rises, feeding it energy makes it colder:

C=d(Mc2)dT=−8πGM2kBℏc=−2SC = \frac{d(Mc^2)}{dT} = -\frac{8 \pi G M^2 k_\mathrm{B}}{\hbar c} = -2SC=dTd(Mc2)​=−ℏc8πGM2kB​​=−2S

A negative heat capacity is not as exotic as it sounds; stars have one too. A star that loses energy contracts and gets hotter, which is why its fusion regulates itself. But it means a black hole cannot sit in stable balance with a bath around it. Any small fluctuation grows: a hole slightly colder than its surroundings absorbs, grows and cools further. Every one of these oddities comes from the same fact. Gravity always attracts and cannot be screened, so a system bound by its own gravity cannot simply be made twice as big with twice as much of everything.

There is a further caution. Temperature belongs to a system in equilibrium, and a merger is not one. Before it there are two settled holes, each with its own temperature. After it there is one, settling over a few milliseconds. In between, nothing is stationary, and the hole has no temperature at all. The area theorem still holds there, and an entropy can still be assigned to the horizon, but the rest of the dictionary is undefined. A merger is a passage between two equilibria through a region where the theory of equilibrium says nothing, and it is over in a fraction of a second.

A contradiction, not a gap

If a black hole radiates, it eventually evaporates. Hawking’s calculation says the radiation is exactly thermal: fixed by the mass and spin alone, carrying no trace of what fell in. Burn a book, and the smoke and ash still carry its words, scrambled but in principle recoverable; quantum mechanics never destroys information. Drop the same book into a black hole and wait, and by Hawking’s calculation what remains is featureless radiation and then nothing.

Don Page sharpened this in 1993. If the evaporation obeys quantum mechanics, the entanglement entropy of the radiation must rise at first, then turn over once the hole has lost about half its entropy, and fall to zero by the end. Hawking’s calculation gives a curve that only rises.

Since 2019 calculations in gravity itself, including new geometries called replica wormholes and islands, do reproduce the turnover. That is real progress: the geometry knows about quantum mechanics. What is still missing is the mechanism, how the information actually gets out, and what someone falling in meets at a horizon that must somehow carry it. None of this can be tested with a gravitational-wave detector, and it would be dishonest to suggest otherwise. It belongs here because it is why black holes matter far beyond astronomy.

Two of them

Einstein found the wave solutions of his equations in 1916, within months of writing them down. Ripples in the geometry itself, travelling at the speed of light, stretching space one way and squeezing it the other as they pass. He then spent twenty years unsure they were real. In 1936 he and Nathan Rosen sent Physical Review a paper arguing that gravitational waves did not exist. The anonymous referee found an error; Einstein, unused to anonymous refereeing, withdrew the paper in indignation and never published in the journal again. He found the mistake himself and reversed the conclusion. The physics was settled in the late 1950s and 1960s, and in the data by Hulse and Taylor’s binary pulsar, which The Straightest Path followed.

Why it takes something lopsided

Light is made by any charge that shakes: the simplest radiation is a dipole. Gravity cannot do that. Its monopole is the total mass, which is conserved, so a sphere that pulses radiates nothing; that was Birkhoff’s theorem in the last article. Its dipole changes at the rate of the total momentum, which is also conserved. The first thing that can change and radiate is the quadrupole: the shape of the mass, how stretched it is and in which direction.

A pair of masses in orbit is the ideal quadrupole. Seen from above, the pair looks the same after half an orbit, so the wave comes out at twice the orbital frequency. How much power comes out is set by Einstein’s quadrupole formula, and its scale is

c5G≈3.6×1052 W\frac{c^5}{G} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{52}\ \text{W}Gc5​≈3.6×1052 W

with the power the binary actually radiates a small fraction of that, growing steeply as the orbit tightens: the inverse fifth power of the separation for a circular orbit (Peters and Mathews, 1963). That large number in the denominator is why gravitational waves are so faint from anything ordinary. Only two black holes in their last orbits come close to it. At its peak GW150914 radiated about 3.6 × 10⁴⁹ W, more than all the stars in the observable universe give off as light (LIGO and Virgo, 2016).

What it means.

A gravitational wave needs a mass distribution that changes shape, not one that only moves or pulses. The c⁵ in the denominator is why only the most compact, fastest systems are loud.

Two polarisations, 45° apart

A passing wave does not push anything along its direction of travel. It stretches space across it in one direction while squeezing the perpendicular one, then reverses. There are exactly two independent ways to do that, called plus and cross, turned 45° from each other rather than the 90° that separates the polarisations of light. That angle is the signature of a field of spin two.

It is also why a detector has two perpendicular arms. A wave from overhead lengthens one arm while shortening the other, and an interferometer is built to notice exactly that difference. The real stretch is a strain of about 10⁻²¹. Over LIGO’s 4 km arms that is a few times 10⁻¹⁸ m, about 420 times smaller than a proton. How such a signal is pulled out of noise is the subject of Hidden in the Noise; how the chirp mass is read off the rising sweep, of From Strain to Source.

The part no approximation survives

A merger has three parts, and each needs its own mathematics. The inspiral is slow enough to expand in powers of v/c, and post-Newtonian theory handles it analytically over thousands of cycles. The ringdown is a single distorted hole settling, which perturbation theory handles again. Between them are about ten milliseconds in which v/c reaches about one half, the expansion fails, and there is no single stationary hole to perturb. The only thing to do is to solve Einstein’s equations on a computer.

Hahn and Lindquist first tried in 1964, with two holes colliding head-on. The codes ran, after a fashion, for four decades, and crashed before the holes met. The reasons are worth understanding, because they were not a shortage of computing power.

Turning spacetime into a simulation

The field equations treat space and time together. A computer needs data now and a rule for a moment later. So spacetime is cut into a stack of slices of space, an idea Richard Arnowitt, Stanley Deser and Charles Misner put into its standard form around 1960.

ds2=−α2c2dt2+γij(dxi+βidt)(dxj+βjdt)ds^2 = -\alpha^2 c^2 dt^2 + \gamma_{ij} \left(dx^i + \beta^i dt\right)\left(dx^j + \beta^j dt\right)ds2=−α2c2dt2+γij​(dxi+βidt)(dxj+βjdt)

Each slice carries its geometry γij and how it is bent within spacetime. The lapse α and shift βi are not physics at all; they are choices, how the slices and the coordinates are laid down, and they can be chosen freely. Four of the ten field equations turn out to contain no time derivatives. They are constraints that every slice must satisfy, and if they hold on the first slice the exact equations keep them true. A computer’s rounding errors do not.

Equations that are right and still useless

The remaining equations, written the obvious way, are correct and nearly useless. They are only weakly hyperbolic: two of their signal speeds coincide without two independent directions to carry them. A system like that turns small errors in short waves into large ones, roughly in proportion to how short the waves are. A grid fine enough to carry shorter waves carries larger errors.

That was the signature the field kept seeing and kept misreading: refine the grid and the code died sooner. Over two crossings of the grid, going from 64 points to 512 makes the toy’s weak system amplify its errors 8 times more. The full equations are worse, because a weakly hyperbolic system is not stable against the small extra terms they carry, and those can make the growth exponential. For years it was blamed on resolution, boundaries and coordinates, on everything but the form of the equations.

What it means.

The fix was never a bigger computer. It was to write the same physics as a different, strongly hyperbolic system.

The rewrite that worked came in two forms. Masaru Shibata and Takashi Nakamura in 1995, and Thomas Baumgarte and Stuart Shapiro in 1998, split the geometry of each slice into a conformal factor and the rest, and promoted a combination of its derivatives to a variable of its own; the result, called BSSN, is strongly hyperbolic. Frans Pretorius took another route: he imposed a wave equation on the coordinates themselves, which turns Einstein’s equations into ten coupled wave equations that are well posed from the start.

Coordinates that survive, and a singularity on the grid

Then came the choices that nobody derived. The slicing called 1 + log slows the advance of time wherever the curvature grows, so the slices creep up on each singularity without reaching it while running normally far away. A companion condition on the shift spreads the grid points away from the holes. Both were found by trying things until the codes stopped crashing.

Inside each horizon the curvature still diverges, and a grid cannot hold an infinity. Pretorius cut a region inside each horizon out of the grid, which is legitimate because nothing inside can affect anything outside. In 2005 his code followed a full orbit, the merger and the ringdown. Within months two other groups, at the University of Texas at Brownsville and at NASA’s Goddard centre, independently found the opposite trick: leave the singularities in, factor out their worst part by hand, and with these coordinates simply let them move across the grid. Nobody had predicted that this moving puncture method would work. It did, and the explanation came afterwards.

Two practical problems remained. Two holes cannot simply be placed side by side, because adding two solutions of a non-linear equation does not give a solution; the constraints have to be solved for a genuine two-hole slice, and whatever is left over radiates away in the first orbit as a burst of unphysical junk radiation that is thrown out. And the orbit set up is never quite circular, so runs are repeated, adjusting the starting speeds, until the leftover eccentricity is small enough. Each attempt is a supercomputer job. The wave itself is read off far from the holes, from the part of the curvature that carries radiation, on spheres at several radii, and extrapolated outwards to where a detector would be.

What comes out

The simulations do not track the event horizon, which can only be found once the whole future is known. They track the apparent horizon, the outermost surface from which light cannot get further out at that moment. What they show is worth stating carefully: a common horizon appears around both holes before the two individual horizons have gone, and it is already there when the wave peaks. It forms badly distorted, like a peanut, and the relaxing of that distortion is the ringdown.

Thousands of simulations have since been distilled into formulae that everyone now uses. For two equal holes without spin, the hole left behind has 0.952 of the starting mass and spins at 0.69: 4.8 % of the mass leaves as waves, against the 24 % the area theorem would allow a hole of that spin.

That final spin is a near-constant of nature. Merge two equal holes that do not spin, and the remnant always spins at about 0.69, because the angular momentum the orbit still has when the holes plunge is what it is. GW250114’s remnant, at 0.68, is where it should be.

One output nobody expected was a kick. Waves carry momentum as well as energy, and an unequal or misaligned binary sends them out unevenly, so the remnant recoils. For holes without spin the recoil peaks at about 175 km/s (González and colleagues, 2007). With spins lying in the orbital plane and pointing opposite ways, simulations found recoils of up to about 4000 km/s (Campanelli and colleagues, 2007): faster than the escape speed of almost any galaxy. A merger in the right configuration throws its remnant out of the galaxy that made it. No analytic calculation had predicted it. It was found by solving the equations.

Why forty years were worth it

The templates that find signals are tuned on these simulations; without them, matched filtering loses the loudest part of the signal. GW150914 was heavy enough that little of its inspiral fell in the detectors’ band, so it was found largely through the merger the codes had learned to model. The fits for the final mass and spin are how every ringdown test is set up, since a test of the Kerr prediction needs the Kerr prediction. And the picture of the merger itself, the common horizon forming early and its relaxing being the ringdown, came from nowhere else.

Where the ringing begins

The ringdown is where the hole says what it is. Its tones are fixed by its mass and spin alone, like the notes of a bell fixed by its shape, and measuring two of them is a test of the Kerr geometry that A Bell That Weighs Itself introduces. The difficulty is when to start listening. Too early and the merger’s non-linear mess is still in the signal; too late and the tones have faded into the noise. There is no calculation that says where the ringing begins, and how that choice moves the answer is the subject of What the Wrong Model Knows.

A geometry, two numbers and a sound

Gravity turned out not to be a force but the shape of spacetime. The purest objects that shape allows are fixed by a mass and a spin, their horizons can only grow, and that growth is an entropy, the largest store of it in the universe. When two of them collide they ring, and the ringing is fixed by what they became. Every step of that was worked out on paper long before anyone could check it: Einstein in 1915, Schwarzschild from the front in 1916, Kerr in 1963, Hawking in 1971. The instruments came a century later and found the paper had been right, in the one place where there was every reason to expect it to fail.

The remarkable part is not that we can hear black holes. It is that we knew what they would sound like.
